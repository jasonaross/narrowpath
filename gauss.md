# The Geometry of the Primes, from the Standpoint of Gauss

A series of three classes contrasting two families of primes, from the standpoint of geometry, the modulus, and the complex domain.

### Background

Besides 2, all prime numbers are odd. We can divide them into two groups based on the remainder when you divide them by 4. All odd primes have a remainder of either 1 or 3. We can call these the $4n+1$ and $4n+3$ primes.

* Examples of the $4n+1$ primes include 5 (which is $4\times1 + 1$), 17 (which is $4\times4 + 1$) and 29 (which is $4\times7+1$).

* Examples of the $4n+3$ primes include 7 (which is $4\times1 + 3$), 23 (which is $4\times5 + 3$) and 43 (which is $4\times10+3$).

There are many differences between these two families, which will be the focus of these classes. First, a word about Carl Gauss's approach to modular arithmetic.

Gauss considered what numbers would be like if, instead of existing on an infinite number line (or an infinitely large circle, if you prefer) to a finite circle, where certain numbers would be functionally equivalent. For example, a clock has hours from 1 to 12. There is no difference, from the standpoint of looking at a clock, whether it has been 3 hours, 15 hours, 27 hours, or 39. You can add or subtract 12 (known as the _modulus_) without changing your location on the clock. Gauss found many exciting properties when considering prime numbers as moduli. Let's take an example.

As an example, in the world of modulus 5—written (mod 5) for example, any two numbers whose difference is a multiple of 5 are considered congruent. Here are some example congruences, where the $\equiv$ symbol is read "congruent to."

* $3 \equiv 8 \equiv 13 \equiv 17$ (mopd 5).

* We can even go less than zero. For example, $3 \equiv -2 \equiv -7$ (mod 5).

* We can note that $4 \equiv -1$ (mod 5), meaning that $2\times 2 \equiv 4 \equiv -1$ (mod 5), meaning that $2^2 \equiv -1$ (mod 5) and therefore we could say that $2 \equiv \sqrt{-1}$. This means that the complex domain is an inherent part of the world of modulus 5.

### The Characteristics We'll Examine from Gauss's Standpoint

There are several characteristic differences between the $4n+1$ and $4n+3$ families of the primes. These are the ones we will examine:

* _All_ $4n+1$ primes can be written as the sum of two square numbers. _No_ $4n+3$ primes can be written as the sum of two square numbers.

* _All_ $4n+1$ primes have a $\sqrt{-1}$. _No_ $4n+3$ primes have a number that is equivalent to $\sqrt{-1}$.

**We will prove these two properties, and find that they are actually connected.** If we have extra time, we can look at (but not demonstrate) another:

* If you choose two primes, each one might or might not be a square number in the other modulus. For example, for 3 and 13, $4^2 \equiv 3$ (mod 13), making 3 a square number in modulus 13, and $13 \equiv 1$ (mod 3), making 13 a square number in modulus 3. But for 5 and 13, neither is a square in the other's modulus. And for 3 and 11, 3 _is_ a square modulus 11, but 11 _is not_ a square modulus 3.

  * If either prime is $4n+1$, then the two will agree: either both are squares, or neither is a square in the other's modulus.
 
  * If _both_ primes are $4n+3$, then they will always disagree: one will be a square from the modulus of the other, but not the other way around.
